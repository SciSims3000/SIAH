Assessment in the Age of AI

Learning can range widely. Assessment needs an anchor.

Executive summary

Generative artificial intelligence has created a major challenge for assessment, but it has not created the underlying problem. Students have always had access to assistance that could obscure the boundary between their own learning and the work of others: copied homework, excessive assistance, plagiarism, purchased assignments and contract cheating.

AI has made the easy way extraordinarily easy.

Assistance that once required money, contacts, time or specialist expertise is now available almost instantly. The sustainable response cannot depend primarily on detecting AI or recreating a pre-AI world. Assessment design instead needs to distinguish between two questions: what activities help students learn, and what evidence allows a teacher to conclude that the student has learnt?

Research, drafting, collaboration, digital portfolios, experimentation, feedback and appropriate AI use can remain valuable learning activities. The resulting polished product, however, is becoming less reliable as standalone evidence of individual achievement.

Do not confuse the product of learning with the evidence of learning.

The response proposed here is validation: a sufficiently controlled opportunity for students to demonstrate the knowledge, understanding and skills represented by work undertaken in less-controlled circumstances. Validation does not mean turning every task into another examination. Validation becomes the anchor.

Research → collaboration → AI → experimentation → feedback → drafting → portfolios → reflection

AI acknowledgement — how this paper was developed

Artificial intelligence was not simply used to proofread this paper. AI participated substantially in its development. The teacher began with professional experience: Assessment Validation Tasks, concerns about take-home research, observations about digital portfolios and recollections of earlier assignment outsourcing.

The teacher introduced problems, challenged propositions, supplied professional context, accepted some ideas, rejected others and repeatedly redirected the argument. ChatGPT helped research evidence, locate historical parallels, test propositions, suggest terminology, organise ideas and generate alternative formulations. Some ideas originated with the teacher, some with ChatGPT, and others emerged through interaction.

A finished digital artefact reveals surprisingly little about this process. A timestamp can show when text appeared, but not who did the thinking. The provenance of an artefact is not the same as evidence of learning. The more meaningful question is not simply “Who typed the words?” but “Who can demonstrate the understanding they represent?”

1. The problem did not begin with AI

Public discussion sometimes treats generative AI as though it broke an assessment system that previously gave reliable evidence of independent work. That environment never really existed. Students could copy homework, receive excessive help, plagiarise, buy work or ask another person to complete it. The 2014 MyMaster contract-cheating case and later TEQSA guidance document this pre-AI problem. Students seeking shortcuts is not new. What has changed is the friction involved.

2. What AI actually changes

Earlier forms of inappropriate assistance usually had barriers: money, a willing helper, access to someone else's work, significant search effort, or enough knowledge to adapt copied material. Generative AI can reduce these barriers to a prompt. It can research, plan, draft, rewrite, calculate, analyse, code, graph, critique and respond to marking criteria.

Integration matters most. AI is increasingly embedded inside documents, portfolios, learning platforms, browsers and productivity software. Version history may show that text appeared under a student's login, without demonstrating that the student cognitively generated it.

A timestamp shows when something happened in a file. It does not show who did the thinking.

3. Pandora's box is open

Some situations should prohibit AI, and students still need explicit instruction about authorised use and acknowledgement. But prohibition and AI detection alone cannot provide assessment validity. If validity depends on proving that AI did not contribute to an unsupervised digital artefact, the architecture is fragile. The durable question is: what did the student learn, and what evidence supports that judgement?

4. Activity is not evidence

Students research, discuss, practise, experiment, collaborate, draft, receive feedback, make mistakes and reflect because these activities help them learn. Schools have sometimes assumed that the artefact generated by a valuable activity must also be good assessment evidence. AI exposes the weakness in that assumption.

Learning activity → product or process → validation → assessment judgement

5. Research still matters

Research should not become collateral damage. Finding, evaluating and synthesising information is more important when information is easy to generate. Students must examine authorship, evidence, corroboration, methodology, bias and omitted information; test whether conclusions follow from evidence; and identify fabricated citations or misleading summaries. Research can continue at home. The polished report alone should no longer be assumed to prove mastery.

6. Digital portfolios: from authentication device to learning journey

Portfolios can capture planning, research, prototypes, practical work, feedback, errors, redesign and reflection. They become problematic when treated as proof of independent authorship. AI can assist with nearly every element, and timestamps cannot reliably establish who performed the underlying cognitive work.

The portfolio documents the journey. Validation establishes whether the student understands the journey.

7. Linking the portfolio and the AVT

A promising model deliberately designs the portfolio and Assessment Validation Task together. A STEAM project might use these phases:

Define → Research → Ideate → Plan → Create → Test → Analyse → Refine → Communicate → Reflect

Students know the kinds of knowledge and question families they may need to address, and that any phase may be selected, but not which phases their AVT will sample. Five equivalent versions might sample Research–Testing– Reflection; Define–Analysis–Communication; Ideation–Planning–Refinement; Research–Creation–Analysis; or Planning–Testing–Communication. Versions need comparable cognitive demand, not identical phase combinations.

8. Whole-process retrieval

Before responding to sampled phases, every student can reconstruct the project in a concise table: Phase; What I did; Important decision; Evidence used; What changed; What I learnt. Its purpose is retrieval, not lengthy prose.

Portfolio = complete journey · Summary table = whole-process retrieval · AVT = unpredictable sample · Teacher judgement = portfolio evidence + validated understanding

9. Why unpredictability matters

Validation should not become another scriptable task. When any phase may be sampled, students need sufficient ownership of the complete process. This does not require trick questions: students should know the question families. What remains unknown is the sampling. The purpose is not surprise. The purpose is valid sampling.

10. Validation: the assessment anchor

Validation is a controlled opportunity for a student to demonstrate the knowledge, understanding and skills represented by work completed in less-controlled circumstances. Students should still research, collaborate, experiment, seek feedback, use technology appropriately, make errors and explore unexpected directions. When a consequential judgement is made, it must connect to sufficiently trustworthy evidence.

The anchor does not replace the journey. It allows us to trust where the learning is.

11. A validation toolkit for teachers

  • Short supervised validation: unseen questions sampling core learning.
  • Viva voce: unscripted questions about choices, evidence, limitations and changes.
  • Cold retrieval: retrieve central knowledge without the original work.
  • Transfer task: apply learning in a new context.
  • Source defence: explain a source's contribution, credibility and limitations.
  • Random portfolio sampling: explain teacher-selected phases or entries.
  • Practical demonstration: demonstrate the skill claimed by the work.
  • Live modification: respond to new evidence or a changed constraint.
  • Explain your own work: unpack a selected graph, calculation, design or conclusion.
  • Micro-presentation plus questioning: a short explanation followed by unscripted questions.

12. Validation should sample, not duplicate

Teacher workload is central. A 2,000-word report does not require another 2,000-word response. It might require three short questions, one transfer problem, a five-minute viva, or two randomly selected portfolio phases. Validation should be proportionate to the claim about achievement.

13. The renewed importance of controlled assessment

As learning becomes more open and technologically assisted, controlled assessment becomes more valuable—not because every task should become an examination, but because it reduces uncertainty about identity, assistance, resources, timing and individual performance. The important question is not merely whether take-home assessment should disappear, but what evidence should replace the function its products once served.

14. Queensland and the value of mixed evidence

Queensland operated senior schooling for more than 40 years with 100% school-based assessment supported by moderation and quality assurance. Its current QCE system combines three internal assessments with one externally set and marked assessment. External assessment contributes 25% in most General subjects and 50% in Mathematics and Science.

This does not prove one system superior. It demonstrates the value of mixed evidence: internal assessment can capture extended inquiry, projects and practical work, while external assessment offers common, controlled conditions.

15. Why the HSC still matters

The HSC examination may appear unlike many professional environments, but that confuses its purpose. Its value lies in providing a strong evidence environment. For most examined NSW Board Developed Courses, school assessment and the external examination each contribute 50%. The exam offers controlled resources, individual responses, common questions and timing, external marking and fewer opportunities for invisible assistance. It is a large-scale validation anchor that complements richer school-based assessment.

16. A practical portfolio–AVT model

  1. Establish the phases. Define a consistent project structure.
  2. Scaffold each phase. Ask what students attempted, what influenced them, what failed and what changed.
  3. Explain validation from the beginning. Students know any phase may be sampled and that they must explain decisions and learning.
  4. Produce parallel AVTs. Use equivalent versions sampling different phase combinations.
  5. Begin with whole-process retrieval. Every student completes the concise project-summary table.
  6. Sample selected phases. Ask deeper questions about the assigned phases.
  7. Include transfer. Add a new constraint or contradictory evidence so students must use, not reproduce, their learning.

17. Implications for AI use

Validation may let schools become less defensive about appropriate AI. Students might use AI to generate search terms, explain difficult ideas, identify alternative perspectives, critique arguments, practise questions or rehearse a viva. The educational questions become: what did the tool help you learn, and can you demonstrate that learning?

18. A practical model for faculties

  1. What are students meant to learn? Identify the target knowledge, understanding and skills.
  2. Which parts are primarily learning activities? Research, drafting, collaboration, experimentation and feedback may belong here.
  3. What does the submitted product actually prove? Be cautious about what can genuinely be inferred.
  4. Which claims need validation? Identify those requiring stronger individual evidence.
  5. What is the smallest effective validation? Choose an approach proportionate to the assessment claim.

Teach → Explore → Produce → Validate → Judge

19. Recommendations

For teachers

Keep teaching research, writing, inquiry, portfolios, reflection, collaboration and responsible AI use. Add proportionate validation rather than assuming an unsupervised artefact is sufficient evidence.

For faculties

Audit tasks that rely on assumptions of unsupervised authorship. Retain rich activities, link major work to validation, develop parallel versions and include transfer.

For schools and systems

Develop shared validation principles and preserve multiple forms of evidence. Rich internal assessment and controlled examinations serve different, complementary purposes.

For students

You may have extraordinary tools for learning. You must still be able to demonstrate what you know and can do.

Conclusion

Artificial intelligence has not destroyed assessment. It has exposed assumptions that were already fragile. The response should not be to abandon research, portfolios, extended inquiry, collaboration or creativity, nor to reconstruct a world without AI. Learning can be rich, open and technologically enabled while assessment still produces defensible judgements. The connection is validation.

Learning can range widely. Assessment needs an anchor. Validation is that anchor.
